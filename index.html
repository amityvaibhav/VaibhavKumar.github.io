I study how landscapes dry out, and how fast.

PERUGIA, ITALY·PREVIOUSLY TAINAN AND NEW DELHI·PhD, NATIONAL CHENG KUNG UNIVERSITY

Flash droughts can turn a wet field into a failing crop within weeks. I develop statistical, machine-learning and process-oriented methods to detect them from satellites, understand what drives them, and trace how hydroclimatic anomalies propagate through the land–atmosphere–hydrology continuum.

I work at CNR – Istituto di Geoscienze (CNR-GEO) in Perugia, in the Hydrology Next group, on the Open-Earth-Monitor Cyberinfrastructure project and the EUMETSAT H SAF programme. Before Italy I worked in Taiwan and India, on drought monitoring and regional climate modelling.

Research

Five threads in my work

My interests are climate modelling, hydroclimatic extremes, rare events, drought and soil moisture. I integrate Earth observation, in-situ measurements, reanalysis products and numerical simulations to characterise how these interact, from regional to continental scale.

01

Flash and conventional droughts

Flash droughts are defined by speed rather than severity, which makes them invisible to tools built for slow, seasonal drought. My doctoral work established how both kinds differ in characteristics, drivers, patterns and trends; I now extend that from Asia to Europe.

ASCAT · ESA CCI · ERA5-Land · GloFAS

02

Soil moisture as the signal

Root-zone soil moisture is where drought becomes physical. I build harmonised, percentile-based frameworks across satellite products, reanalyses and models, so that an event detected in one dataset means the same as one detected in another.

Percentile diagnostics · Rare-event statistics

03

Drivers and teleconnections

Droughts over India and Taiwan respond to sea surface temperature in the Indian Ocean, to ENSO and to the Indian Ocean Dipole. I quantify those links, including through GNSS-derived precipitable water vapour.

GNSS PWV · SST indices · TerraClimate

04

Climate modelling and projection

From dynamical downscaling with WRF over the Indian monsoon region to event-based characterisation of twenty-first-century drought, I work on what models can and cannot tell us about extremes. That now extends to Geo-AI and foundation models such as TerraMind, which learn across many Earth observation modalities at once.

WRF · Geo-AI · TerraMind

05

Climate intervention and drought risk

If solar geoengineering were deployed, what would happen to drought? I examine that question for India and for Europe, treating deliberate intervention as another forcing whose hydrological consequences deserve the same scrutiny as warming itself.

GeoMIP-style scenarios · CMIP6 · NASA-NEX-GDDP

A result

Where flash droughts strike over India is robust across datasets. How often, how fast and how hard they strike is not.

Comparing root-zone soil moisture products, event dynamics turn out to be far more consistent than event counts, which means the choice of product matters for monitoring but less for identifying where the risk lies.

    Consistency and Divergence of Root-Zone Soil Moisture Products for Flash Drought Monitoring over India

  2. 2025

    Rapid Mapping of Rainfall-Induced Landslide Using Multi-Temporal Satellite Data

    M. A. Aman, H.-J. Chu, S. R. Partra, V. Kumar · Remote Sensing 17(8), 1407

    A semi-automated algorithm pairing Sentinel-2 NDVI time series with GPM-IMERG rainfall to map rainfall-triggered landslides, reaching an F1-score of 0.82 across ten cases worldwide, and finding slides the manual inventories had missed.

    doi:10.3390/rs17081407

  3. 2024

    Impacts of sea surface temperature variability in the Indian Ocean on drought conditions over India during ENSO and IOD events

    V. Kumar, H.-J. Chu, A. Anand · Journal of Marine Science and Engineering 12(1), 136

    Warm anomalies in the tropical Indian Ocean weaken the monsoon at its onset, cutting moisture reaching India. Tracing the 2009 and 2015 droughts through rainfall, temperature, soil moisture and chlorophyll fluorescence shows the land signal following the ocean, though vegetation suffers less than the water deficit alone would suggest.

    doi:10.3390/jmse12010136

  4. 2024

    Seasonal drought severity identification using a modified multivariate index: a case study of the Indo-Gangetic Plains in India

    V. Kumar, H.-J. Chu · Journal of Hydrology

    A composite index combining standardised precipitation, soil moisture and solar-induced chlorophyll fluorescence, which identified drought hotspots across the Indo-Gangetic Plains more sharply than its single-variable components. Recognised with a publication award at NCKU.

    doi:10.1016/j.jhydrol.2024.130632

  5. 2024

    Relative importance of VECTRI model parameters in the malaria disease transmission and prevalence

    R. S. Parihar, V. Kumar, A. Anand, P. K. Bal, A. Thapliyal · International Journal of Biometeorology 68(3), 495–509

    A sensitivity analysis of the VECTRI malaria model over Odisha, one of India’s most endemic states, identifying degree-days for parasite development, the minimum temperature for larval survival and pond water loss as the parameters that matter most.

    doi:10.1007/s00484-023-02607-z

  6. 2023

    Spatio-temporal consistency and inconsistency of meteorological and agricultural drought identification: a case study of India

    V. Kumar, H.-J. Chu · Remote Sensing Applications: Society and Environment

    Rainfall deficit and soil moisture deficit do not always agree about when and where a drought is happening.

    Read on ScienceDirect →

  7. 2019

    Numerical modeling of the dynamics of malaria transmission in a highly endemic region of India

    R. S. Parihar, P. K. Bal, V. Kumar, S. K. Mishra, S. Sahany, P. Salunke, S. K. Dash · Scientific Reports 9, 11903 · open access

    Calibrating the VECTRI model over Odisha for 2000 to 2013 shows transmission peaking where monthly mean temperature sits near 28 to 29 °C and rainfall between about 200 and 360 mm, with the heaviest burden in forest and hill districts rather than the wettest coastal ones.

Beyond drought

I keep an active collaboration with atmospheric scientists in India on aviation meteorology, applying machine learning to winter fog forecasting at Kolkata, Patna and Guwahati airports, and on monsoon dynamics. Earlier work with IIT Delhi applied the VECTRI model to climate-driven malaria transmission. The common thread is the same one that runs through the drought work: taking a physical system, finding the signal that matters, and making it usable by someone who has to decide something.
